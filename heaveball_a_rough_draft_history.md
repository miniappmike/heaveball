# Heaveball: A Rough Draft History

## 2001: The Boston Intramural League (The Core)
It all started on a chewed-up stretch of grass on the edge of Boston in 2001. A ragtag crew of college kids and neighborhood teens—burned out on slow, overly polished campus sports—wanted something faster, rougher, and built for real athletic grit. 

They didn't have big budgets or official sponsors. All they had were a couple of beat-up balls, four corner quadrants marked out on the grass, and a central runway leading to a single shared ring where big hits and daring throws decided everything. They called it **Heaveball**. Because players were slamming into each other on real turf and grass, the early league threw together whatever tough gear they already owned from skating and street hockey: skate helmets, knee and elbow pads, and protective vests worn underneath long-sleeve jerseys. 

What started as an informal intramural league quickly turned into an underground obsession. The founding crew pitched it to every friend, roommate, and pickup athlete they knew—building a culture of "violent friendliness," where hard collisions and diving saves were simply how buddies earned each other's respect.

## The Trail Backwards: How a Group of Buddies Unearthed It
The Boston crew didn't actually invent the game's blueprint from scratch—they stumbled onto it by accident. 

During the league's first chaotic season, one of the founding players was digging through old family trunks and secondhand maritime books when a faded ink sketch slipped out. Drawn on weathered paper was a four-quadrant layout, a central lane, and a circle right in the middle—a diagram that matched the exact field setup they were arguing over on the Boston grass.

Hooked by the coincidence, the buddies started piecing together the trail. They dug through old shipping logs, harbor notes, and forgotten athletic histories, slowly realizing their ragtag Boston league wasn't a random invention. They had accidentally unearthed a lost contact sport that had vanished for centuries before landing right in their laps.

## The 1600s Discovery: Rough Athletes and Early Performative Showmen
Following that paper trail back to the 1600s led to a gritty world far outside North America—a time when rough laborers, off-duty warriors, and early performative athletes put on raw displays of strength, speed, and toughness to blow off steam.

Back then, there were no skate helmets, no pads, and no long-sleeve jerseys. Players went into the dirt bare-headed and unprotected, trading bruised ribs and torn clothes while loud crowds watched heavy-set "anchors" hold the center ground against fast runners diving for the ring. For the fighters and showmen of that era, the game was equal parts contest, spectacle, and camaraderie—a way to hit hard, test nerve, and put on a show without turning a friendly rivalry into real bloodshed.

Over the next three hundred years, the game slipped out of sight as laws changed and newer, more formal sports took over, leaving behind only scattered scraps of rules and field shapes. Then, in 2001, a handful of edgy buddies in Boston dug up enough of the puzzle to bring the whole thing roaring back to life on the grass.
