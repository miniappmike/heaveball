# Heaveball

Heaveball is a spectator-first fictional sports league built around a physical sport for everyday athletes. The playable milestone is an autonomous match that fans can watch, pause and use to understand the sport. The longer-term goal is a league fans can follow between games, with recognizable clubs, persistent players and reporting grounded in what happened on the field.

## Spread forecast

The pregame spread is a fitted model, not the overall-rating difference. Each club's expected points start from a base of about 21 and add weighted contributions from its own 12 trait averages, the opponent's trait averages, its offensive playbook, the opponent's defensive scheme and its own defensive tempo. The spread is the difference in expected points, rounded to the half point. The total is their sum. Win probability treats the final margin as normally distributed around the spread with an 11.6-point standard deviation.

The weights were refitted with ridge regression on 960 headless full matches after the softer short-pass change. On held-out matches the model explained about 54% of the variance in final margin, against about 18% for the overall-rating difference alone. Favorites given 80–90% won 89% of the time and favorites given 90% or more won 96% of the time. Tactics carry much of the signal. Carrier pressure and Receiver denial lower the opponent's expected points the most, Star focus and Ring guard concede the most, and Ring guard also slows its own offense. Throw Accuracy, Agility and Strength are the most predictive traits. Refit the weights in the view JavaScript whenever scoring, tactics or player generation change materially.

## Field and scoring

Two teams play two 20-minute halves on a 50-by-34-yard field. Both attack one four-yard ring in a ten-yard square center zone. Green quadrants permit throwing and running. Yellow runways permit running and handoffs. Every role can traverse the full blue center zone. The central strips belong to neither half. Only Anchors can move beyond them into the opposite half. Off-ball players can cross the dirt, and an airborne pass may travel freely above it. The dirt becomes out of bounds when a carried ball enters it or when a pass, deflection or loose ball touches or rolls onto the surface. The opponent then receives a quadrant reset nearest the exit. More than three consecutive live game-clock seconds unblocked inside the ring still awards the opponent a free run lane and charges the player a penalty. Leaving the ring, getting blocked or becoming tied up in a pile resets the count.

| Points | Play |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | Intercept a pass or recover a fumble in the center. Keep possession. |
| 2 | Carry the ball from outside the center through the ring. |
| 3 | Catch a teammate’s pass in the center and break the ring line under control. |
| 5 | Catch an airborne save kick in the center before it lands. Either team can score. |

## Possessions and restarts

Players run 2% faster. Passes, kicks and loose balls travel 2% slower. Throw distance and Arm Strength determine flight time, while the passer chooses a trajectory for the situation. A **lob** is the standard pass: high-arching, comparatively slow, able to clear defenders and easier to receive. A **line drive** is the quickest, flattest and hardest throw; strong arms use it to hit urgent or quick-swing windows, but its pace makes it substantially harder to catch. A **bounce pass** is slower and easier to handle, and is considered only on a legal same-half path because a bounce on dirt is immediately out of bounds. When teammates are within four yards, they use a gentle underhand toss with a low arc and a longer flight time, including under pressure. At handoff distance they can transfer the ball directly instead. Short hops are exceptionally rare through 16 yards. They become more likely on longer attempts, especially when the passer has lower Arm Strength or Throw Accuracy. Low Accuracy, particularly when Arm Strength exceeds control, can also sail a throw beyond and above the intended receiver. During buildup, carriers can release early when a nearby outlet has separation and the passing lane is clear. Patient possessions, protected leads and designed plays can still call for a longer hold. On drive-and-kick possessions the carrier probes the runway, checks space ahead and fatigue, and retreats to a legal quadrant to pass when the lane closes. Outlet selection considers all roles, including Anchors, using separation, Hands and distance. Players read spacing, run routes on both halves, switch the ball across the field, use short outlets, attack the runway and hand off under pressure. Live carriers search for angles and slow with fatigue. Defenders read the ball, the ring and all eligible opposing receivers, including Anchors. They generally take goal-side positions and update their assignments as the play shifts. Agility and reaction shape jukes. Carriers favor safe throwing angles nearer the blue and can attack an open runway while building possession. When progress stalls or coverage tightens, the offense reads the entry lane and its Anchor matchup again. It can switch to drive-and-kick, a power run, an off-ball block or an Anchor leak before the possession ends. One Anchor can block a receiver’s marker while another draws help on the far side. Anchors remain eligible outlets and handoff options and can join a live pile to add force. Defenders keep a fading estimate of drives and blocks, adjust ring depth and can switch coverage when a block frees a receiver. Routine blocking and coverage exchanges stay visual rather than filling the play-by-play. Quadrant defenders give deep corner outlets more room and shade toward the lane from the ball to the circle. Near-side defenders close from the goal side when a runner commits. Defenders can intercept mid-flight through the crowded middle, bat down an airborne pass or tip it sideways. Height and Jump increase reach, Reaction helps a defender respond in time, and Speed helps close the remaining distance to the ball. Passes can also rebound off a receiver or skip past their hands. The simulation tracks ball height, velocity and spin. The ball is a softball-sized rubber ball on turf, so grass, runway turf and dirt produce visible skips but low restitution and rapid vertical energy loss rather than superball rebounds. Directional player impacts can still redirect it. The view renders a plain white ball and a ground shadow that shifts and softens with height. The ball stays live unless it lands or rolls out or another rule stops play. Throw Accuracy, trajectory and coverage shape completions. Height, Jump, Reaction and Hands give players a small edge on contested catches, and deflections stay live until they touch an out-of-bounds surface. Wide-open catchable balls are dropped about 0.2–1.5% of the time. Most drops come from contested or crowded catch points. Hands and Throw Accuracy moderately affect drops in traffic. On short approaches, carriers are more likely to attack the circle when their available blockers can account for the defenders ahead. Broadcast calls describe the action without naming every pass type; three-point passing scores still show throw distance. ## Play calls and sudden death

- **Airborne rescue.** Regulation schedules two or three attempts, spread across the match. Kicking skill influences the chance of returning the ball in bounds, with a target of 30–40% of attempts. Most live returns go to a quadrant receiver. A loose in-bounds kick stays live. A five-point save requires a catch in the blue and has a game-level chance below 2%.
- **Quick swing.** Four quadrant players provide outlets while the carrier moves the ball through open, unvisited quadrants. Airborne lobs and line drives can change halves over the dirt, while bounce passes remain same-half only. Each receiver can release promptly after catching. If the next lane closes, the team resets into its usual reads. The Quick swing playbook favors this call and some Balanced and Free flow possessions use it too.
- **Corner boomerang.** Five percent of quadrant setups call for a deep same-half outlet and a return pass to the original passer cutting into the blue. The offense can switch back to its usual reads if either leg closes.
- **Anchor leak.** An Anchor shows a lead block, then releases into the blue as a receiver. Balanced and Free Flow carry the call, and the offense can also switch to it mid-possession.
- **Last-step handoff.** Balanced, Runway Power and Free Flow can call a runway attack with a trailing carrier. The exchange comes as the first carrier draws a nearby defender.
- **Three-on-three runway.** A regulation tie goes to sudden death. A coin flip sets first possession, and each side fields three Anchors, a Runner and a Heaver. The offense sends its three Anchors against the three defensive Anchors. Turnovers start the new carrier at the back of their runway. The first score ends the match.
- **One-point ambush.** Defensive coaches decide when to show an opening at the edge of the blue and send one Anchor to jump it. Coaches favor the call when chasing a late deficit and hold it back when protecting a late lead.
- **Restart choices.** Scoring teams favor a quadrant with weaker opposing passing or a favorable defensive matchup and alternate choices. This is part of routine restart strategy.

## Offensive playbooks

| Playbook | Pros | Cons |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Patient passing | Protects the ball, sustains possession and makes the defense survive repeated reads. Safer throws and deliberate holds reward disciplined passers. | Creates fewer scoring chances, can stall against carrier pressure and may consume too much clock while trailing. |
| Balanced | Can answer most coverages by mixing swings, drives, blocks, leaks and handoffs. It has the fewest built-in matchup liabilities. | Does not impose one dominant pressure point. Results rely on the carrier recognizing the right answer and executing it. |
| Runway power | Creates direct ring opportunities, uses size and blocking, and punishes a light or isolated front. | Produces shorter, more physical possessions with extra tackle and fumble exposure. Runway contain and talented tacklers are difficult matchups. |
| Quick swing | Uses very short holds and rapid airborne passes through all four quadrants to stretch packed or slow-shifting defenses. | Every exchange creates another catch or deflection opportunity. Switch coverage can follow the sequence, and bounce passes cannot be used to change halves. |
| Free flow | Lets athletes attack the space they see, stays unpredictable and changes calls when a defense overplays the first action. | Its shared reads are demanding. Misread spacing, aggressive throws and improvised decisions make outcomes more volatile. |

The engine applies these identities to release time, passing risk, run appetite and mid-possession choices. They remain situational rather than automatic: ratings, spacing, score, clock and the opposing scheme still decide the play.

## Defensive schemes

| Scheme | Pros | Cons |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Balanced | Keeps two goal-side helpers, matches dangerous eligible receivers and has no glaring structural weakness. | Does not fully erase an elite runner, a star receiver or rapid ball movement. |
| Ring guard | Puts three defenders near the ring, sharply reduces clean ring entries and creates defensive scoring chances through tackles, tips and interceptions. It is strongest with athletic players and talented tacklers. | Concedes deep outlets. Its defense-first posture also produces shorter offensive possessions and fewer clean offensive chances, so much of its scoring must come from defensive plays. Opponents must patiently move and dissect it. |
| Receiver denial | Gives eligible receivers tight goal-side marks, lowers easy completions and makes the carrier hold or run. | Leaves only one dedicated ring helper. Blocks, handoffs and individual athletic mismatches can break the coverage. |
| Star focus | Denies the best Runner and Heaver, then brackets the more dangerous star near the ball. | Secondary receivers and the far side receive more room. Quick reversals force the help to travel and can punish the extra attention. |
| Passing-lane zone | Crowds anticipated throwing lanes and raises the likelihood of interceptions, tips and batted passes. | Short outlets, patient overloads and quick swings can find seams between zones. It also gives receivers space to settle outside the lane. |
| Carrier pressure | Sends an extra defender to shorten the carrier’s read, hurry throws and create immediate turnovers. | A clean outlet, handoff or broken first tackle gets behind the pressure and leaves less help at the ring. |
| Runway contain | Spreads three helpers across run lanes, funnels direct attacks toward tacklers and is the best structural answer to Runway power. | Gives up deeper quadrant outlets and can be stretched by disciplined passing or legal center relays. |
| Switch coverage | Exchanges assignments quickly against cuts, blocks and swing sequences, preventing one screen from freeing a receiver. | Generates fewer aggressive takeaways and can lose physical isolations. A late or low-IQ switch can still expose the ring. |

The play-by-play identifies each club's scheme and the players targeted by Star focus. Free run lanes and sudden death use their dedicated formations. All active players can still use the full center zone in sudden death.

## Gameplay targets

Regulation game totals can range from 0 to 100 combined points, with most matches targeted between 30 and 60. IQ and Discipline influence a carrier’s estimate of runway space, a passer’s ability to recognize defenders in the passing lane, and a help defender’s choice to switch or stay with a dangerous cutter. Defensive Anchors shade closer to the ring, contact matters on contested receptions and defenders have a modest advantage finishing a tackle. The scoring values still come directly from the play. Possession length depends on opportunity, offensive playbook and defensive identity. Each new team draws a playbook with a baseline aggression and a set of preferred opening calls. Patient passing, Balanced, Free flow and Runway power teams take progressively earlier chances on average, while Quick swing releases the ball fastest. Teams ordinarily circulate through a few safe exchanges before committing to a set play. A near-side outlet helps a covered carrier move the ball before contact. A far-side airborne pass may cross above the dirt, but a bounce pass never changes halves and any ball touching dirt is immediately out of bounds. When the runway closes early, a carrier can circle back into a legal quadrant and throw while play continues. Players favor shorter and clearer lanes during buildup, with an exception for a well-supported run. Each call still affects how quickly the passer reads an outlet and how readily a carrier takes an open lane. Mid-possession adjustments can change that appetite for risk. In the last five minutes, a team trailing by five or more attacks sooner and sends a defender to press the ball. In the last two minutes, a team leading by five or more favors short outlets and uses more clock. Long possessions can still develop when a patient team keeps finding outlets. These targets guide randomized match testing. Fans should be able to see how an open outlet, a strong block or a timely stop changed a possession.

## League and media roadmap

The current browser prototype generates fresh exhibition matches. The planned league layer would retain player and club identities, schedule games and record standings, statistics and season history. Shared broadcasts would reveal events as they air. Recordings of player and ball movement alongside the event log would support replays, highlights and box scores. Pregame stories, recaps and ongoing media coverage would use those same records so a fan could trace a headline to the play that earned it. Persistent seasons, synchronized broadcasts, replay archives and an automated newsroom are future work.
