# Heaveball

Heaveball is a spectator-first fictional sports league built around a physical sport for everyday athletes. The playable milestone is an autonomous match that fans can watch, pause and use to understand the sport. The longer-term goal is a league fans can follow between games, with recognizable clubs, persistent players and reporting grounded in what happened on the field.

Never edit `dist/`; `tools/build.py` regenerates it. The two textured dirt strips above and below the blue center square identify the no-possession area. Compare rule changes against the current handbook and this summary, especially the four-yard ring and the out-of-bounds ruling when a carried or grounded ball enters a dirt strip. For pacing changes, sample full randomized games and report combined scores and possession duration in game-clock seconds. Keep the roadmap below distinct from shipped features. Current playtesting priorities include recognizing off-ball routes, spotting dead balls in the no-possession area and checking that center-zone tackles and runway control affect results.

## Spread forecast

The pregame spread is a fitted model, not the overall-rating difference. Each club's expected points start from a base of about 26 and add weighted contributions from its own 12 trait averages, the opponent's trait averages, its offensive playbook, the opponent's defensive scheme and its own defense. The spread is the difference in expected points, rounded to the half point. The total is their sum. Win probability treats the final margin as normally distributed around the spread with an 11.2-point standard deviation.

The weights were refitted with ridge regression on 1,000 headless full matches after the ball-handling update and the strategy balance pass. Because no tactic now dominates, results are harder to predict than before: on held-out matches the model explains about 24% of the variance in final margin, against about 14% for the overall-rating difference alone. It stays well calibrated; favorites given 80–90% won 85% of the time. Throw Accuracy and Agility are the most predictive traits. Quick swing and Free flow carry the largest offensive bonus, Patient passing and Runway power the smallest, and Passing-lane zone concedes the most of the defenses. Refit the weights in the view JavaScript whenever scoring, tactics or player generation change materially.

## Strategy balance

Every playbook and defense was tuned against randomized simulations so that none decides a game on its own while each keeps a recognizable style. In the final 1,000-match check, teams scored 26.5 to 32 points a game depending on playbook, and each defense's average scoring margin (its own points minus points allowed) landed within about four points of even.

| Strategy | Signature in simulation |
|---|---|
| Patient passing | Longest possessions, about 55% of the clock, fewest possessions and fewest turnovers |
| Balanced | Even mix of carries, passes and 3-point catches |
| Runway power | Most two-point ring carries, fewest 3-point catches, most turnovers |
| Quick swing | Most passes per possession and the highest completion rate |
| Free flow | Most possessions and the widest mix of scoring plays |
| Ring guard | Fewest ring carries allowed; gives the opponent more possessions |
| Receiver denial | Fewest 3-point catches allowed |
| Star focus | Most passes broken up |
| Passing-lane zone | Lane-reading defenders get extra reach for tips while keeping their ring-side shade |
| Carrier pressure | Most tackles and forced turnovers, with a deep, loose back line that concedes when the press is beaten |
| Runway contain | Funnels runs into tacklers and limits 3-point catches |
| Switch coverage | Steady marks with fewer opponent possessions |

The engine exposes `pressGap` and `rushRead` for Carrier pressure, and `laneStep`, `laneCut` and `laneReach` for Passing-lane zone, so these identities can be adjusted without rewriting the defensive logic.

## Field and scoring

Two teams play two 20-minute halves on a 50-by-34-yard field. Both attack one four-yard ring in a ten-yard square center zone. Green quadrants permit throwing and running. Yellow runways permit running and handoffs. Every role can traverse the full blue center zone. The central strips belong to neither half. Only Anchors can move beyond them into the opposite half. Off-ball players can cross the dirt, and an airborne pass may travel freely above it. A carrier who enters the dirt on their own concedes a free run lane. If contact drives a carrier into the dirt, or a pass, deflection or loose ball touches the surface, the opponent takes possession at the nearest quadrant. More than three consecutive live game-clock seconds unblocked inside the ring still awards the opponent a free run lane and charges the player a penalty. Leaving the ring, getting blocked or becoming tied up in a pile resets the count.

| Points | Play |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | Intercept a pass or recover an opponent's fumble in the center. Keep possession. |
| 2 | Carry the ball from outside the center through the ring. |
| 3 | Catch a teammate’s pass in the center and break the ring line under control. |
| 5 | Catch an airborne save kick in the center before it lands. Either team can score. |

## Play calls and sudden death

- **Airborne rescue.** An errant throw keeps its normal flight time. A teammate chases it only if their position, momentum, Speed, Agility and Reaction give them a chance to reach the sideline. The player must be outside the line and within kicking reach of the descending ball. Most errant throws go out. A successful kick starts at the ball’s contact position and height. Kicking skill, reach and body momentum affect the return. Most returns are short, and a clean strike can reach the blue for a rare five-point catch by either team. An uncaught in-bounds return stays live.
- **Quick swing.** The carrier holds the ball briefly and reads the ring before choosing another outlet. A clear lane can bring a quick pass into the blue or a run through the runway. The ball can work between two or three quadrants, go all the way around, or keep moving for up to eight outlet passes (two full trips around the four quadrants). Teams can reverse direction or skip across the field either way between Q2 and Q4 or Q1 and Q3 when the passing lane opens. If the outlets close or the sequence reaches eight exchanges, the team returns to its usual reads. Every playbook can use this tempo when the spacing suits it. It is most common for Quick swing and rare for Runway power.
- **Corner boomerang.** Five percent of quadrant setups call for a deep same-half outlet and a return pass to the original passer cutting into the blue. The offense can switch back to its usual reads if either leg closes.
- **Anchor leak.** An Anchor shows a lead block, then releases into the blue as a receiver. Balanced and Free Flow carry the call, and the offense can also switch to it mid-possession.
- **Last-step handoff.** Balanced, Runway Power and Free Flow can call a runway attack with a trailing carrier. The exchange comes as the first carrier draws a nearby defender.
- **Three-on-three runway.** A regulation tie goes to sudden death. A coin flip sets first possession, and each side fields three Anchors, a Runner and a Heaver. The offense sends its three Anchors against the three defensive Anchors. Turnovers start the new carrier at the back of their runway. The first score ends the match.
- **One-point ambush.** Defensive coaches decide when to show an opening at the edge of the blue and send one Anchor to jump it. Coaches favor the call when chasing a late deficit and hold it back when protecting a late lead.
- **Restart choices.** Scoring teams favor a quadrant with weaker opposing passing or a favorable defensive matchup and alternate choices. This is part of routine restart strategy.

## Offensive playbooks

Each playbook and defense has a short broadcast-style `blurb` shown under its menu in the pregame tactics card (for example, Runway power: "Downhill runs behind big Anchor blocks"). The full pros and cons below stay in each strategy's `description` and appear as a tooltip on the blurb.

| Playbook | Pros | Cons |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Patient passing | Protects the ball, sustains possession and makes the defense survive repeated reads. Safer throws and deliberate holds reward disciplined passers. | Creates fewer scoring chances, can stall against carrier pressure and may consume too much clock while trailing. |
| Balanced | Can answer most coverages by mixing swings, drives, blocks, leaks and handoffs. It has the fewest built-in matchup liabilities. | Does not impose one dominant pressure point. Results rely on the carrier recognizing the right answer and executing it. |
| Runway power | Creates direct ring opportunities, uses size and blocking, and punishes a light or isolated front. | Produces shorter, more physical possessions with extra tackle and fumble exposure. Runway contain and talented tacklers are difficult matchups. |
| Quick swing | Most often uses short holds and quick passes among open quadrants to set up a ring attack by pass or run. | Every exchange creates another catch or deflection opportunity. Switch coverage can follow the sequence, and bounce passes cannot be used to change halves. |
| Free flow | Lets athletes attack the space they see, stays unpredictable and changes calls when a defense overplays the first action. | Its shared reads are demanding. Misread spacing, aggressive throws and improvised decisions make outcomes more volatile. |

The engine applies these identities to release time, passing risk, run appetite and mid-possession choices. They remain situational rather than automatic: ratings, spacing, score, clock and the opposing scheme still decide the play.

## Defensive schemes

| Scheme | Pros | Cons |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Balanced | Keeps two goal-side helpers, matches dangerous eligible receivers and has no glaring structural weakness. | Does not fully erase an elite runner, a star receiver or rapid ball movement. |
| Ring guard | Puts three defenders near the ring, sharply reduces clean ring entries and creates defensive scoring chances through tackles, tips and interceptions. It is strongest with athletic players and talented tacklers. | Concedes deep outlets. Its defense-first posture also produces shorter offensive possessions and fewer clean offensive chances, so much of its scoring must come from defensive plays. Opponents must patiently move and dissect it. |
| Receiver denial | Gives eligible receivers tight goal-side marks, lowers easy completions and makes the carrier hold or run. | Leaves only one dedicated ring helper. Blocks, handoffs and individual athletic mismatches can break the coverage. |
| Star focus | Denies the best Runner and Heaver, then brackets the more dangerous star near the ball. | Secondary receivers and the far side receive more room. Quick reversals force the help to travel and can punish the extra attention. |
| Passing-lane zone | Crowds anticipated throwing lanes and raises the likelihood of interceptions, tips and batted passes. | Short outlets, patient overloads and quick swings can find seams between zones. It also gives receivers space to settle outside the lane. |
| Carrier pressure | Sends an extra defender to shorten the carrier’s read, hurry throws and create immediate turnovers. | A clean outlet, handoff or broken first tackle gets behind the pressure and leaves less help at the ring. |
| Runway contain | Spreads three helpers across run lanes, funnels direct attacks toward tacklers and is the best structural answer to Runway power. | Gives up deeper quadrant outlets and can be stretched by disciplined passing or legal center relays. |
| Switch coverage | Exchanges assignments quickly against cuts, blocks and swing sequences, preventing one screen from freeing a receiver. | Generates fewer aggressive takeaways and can lose physical isolations. A late or low-IQ switch can still expose the ring. |

Each entry aligns its timestamp with a single message column, keeping team-colored player names inline as the text wraps. On a free run lane, the lead Anchor screens the help defender so the Runner can isolate the first tackler. The play starts when the penalty popup clears. Both defenders can adjust their positions before contact. A tired Runner keeps at least 82% of their movement speed on the approach. The tackler can grab and slow the Runner until help arrives, or lunge for a difficult stop at the spot. A Runner can slip a grab, break free before help arrives, or stay up through a missed lunge. ## Gameplay targets

Regulation game totals can range from 0 to 100 combined points, with most matches targeted between 30 and 60. IQ and Discipline influence a carrier’s estimate of runway space, a passer’s ability to recognize defenders in the passing lane, and a help defender’s choice to switch or stay with a dangerous cutter. Defensive Anchors shade closer to the ring, contact matters on contested receptions and defenders have a modest advantage finishing a tackle. The scoring values still come directly from the play. Possession length depends on opportunity, offensive playbook and defensive identity. Each new team draws a playbook with a baseline aggression and a set of preferred opening calls. Runway power reads the runway before committing. Anchor screens open gaps, misdirection handoffs change the runner, and passes can lead a Runner into space or reset the ground attack. A closed lane sends the carrier back to look for an outlet. Patient passing, Balanced, Free flow and Runway power teams take progressively earlier chances on average, while Quick swing releases the ball fastest. Teams ordinarily circulate through a few safe exchanges before committing to a set play. A near-side outlet helps a covered carrier move the ball before contact. A far-side airborne pass may cross above the dirt, but a bounce pass never changes halves and any ball touching dirt is immediately out of bounds. When the runway closes early, a carrier can circle back into a legal quadrant and throw while play continues. Players favor shorter and clearer lanes during buildup, with an exception for a well-supported run. Each call still affects how quickly the passer reads an outlet and how readily a carrier takes an open lane. Mid-possession adjustments can change that appetite for risk. In the last five minutes, a team trailing by five or more attacks sooner and sends a defender to press the ball. In the last two minutes, a team leading by five or more favors short outlets and uses more clock. Long possessions can still develop when a patient team keeps finding outlets. These targets guide randomized match testing. Fans should be able to see how an open outlet, a strong block or a timely stop changed a possession.

## Player markers

A small on-field stamina bar appears under the carrier and any other player below 35% stamina. Green means 70% or higher, yellow means 35% to below 70%, and red means below 35%. Each player has a hidden stamina rating. Heavier players tend to rate a little lower, with individual conditioning varying within each weight class. Sustained running and contact drain stamina. Slower movement and rest recover it, and halftime restores it. Below 55%, fatigue gradually reduces acceleration and speed, with a larger speed effect for ball carriers. Very tired players are slightly more likely to drop a pass in traffic. The free run lane floor still applies before a grab.

Players keep their numbered circles during loose-ball scrambles. Nearby players pursue if they can reach the ball. The others reposition to cover space or offer an outlet for the recovery.

## Calls and stoppages

Scores, penalties, turnovers and ordinary stoppages freeze play in place for 3.5 seconds. A tackle that keeps possession pauses for 2 seconds with no popup, and the tackled player wears a black inner ring until the next possession. Big plays show a large popup in the middle of the field: CENTER! (blue) for 2-, 3- and 5-point scores, TAKEAWAY! (orange) for 1-point center-zone interceptions and fumble recoveries, TURNOVER (red), PENALTY (yellow, with the penalized player in red) and OUT OF BOUNDS (white) when the same team keeps the ball. A loose ball that touches the dirt shows BALL OUT OF BOUNDS. When a pile drives a carrier into the dirt, the badge shows NO POSSESSION ZONE and the opponent restarts in the nearest quadrant. Both show the awarded team and whether it gains or retains possession. Badge subtitles identify the player as Team #number Name, with the name in team color. Pausing holds the popup on screen.

## Penalties

Every penalty awards a free run lane: three seconds unblocked in the ring, a block in the back, a Runner or Heaver crossing past their own half, and a carrier entering the no-possession zone on their own. Contact that drives the carrier into the dirt awards a quadrant reset. Low IQ and Discipline make penalties more likely. Typical rates per match: about 0.7 half crossings, 1–2 no-possession calls, 2–3 ring-time calls and 2–4 blocks in the back.
